Hypothesis (written before building): the first unit of headroom saves the daily amplitude A per hour (from the sinc law), so always-on capacity pays only where A/c̄ > p0/(1-p0), which is rarely true. Novelty check: Bashir et al. (SoCC'24, 'The Sunk Carbon Fallacy') count the *embodied* carbon of idle servers in scheduling metrics; a CACM news piece ('Putting Carbon on the Clock') warns that over-provisioning for flexibility adds embodied carbon; Tröpgen et al. (IEEE Cluster Workshops 2024) report SPECpower idle shares (15.7% in 2017, 25.7% in 2024, as summarised in search results; confirm against the full text). None of them derives the operational break-even. Result: the hypothesis held in shape but the first-unit value is *above* the daily A on real data, because with tiny headroom the virtual battery plans across days (F22's horizon s·φ/h): the LP's p0* for a 1% build (median 26.7%) is about twice the daily law (14.2%). So the law is stated for a finite build, p0*(h) = v(h)/(v(h)+c̄), with the horizon-free bound 1 - c̄/max c (first proof attempt was loose; replaced by a quantile-function argument). Checks: sine first unit = A (LP dual and sinc derivative), LP <= bound in all 369 zone-years, hourly simulation = LP. Caveats: sleep is idealised (no wake-up latency, failover spare ignored), idle shares are x86 CPU servers. Paper: new §'The Battery Has a Standby Load' (proposition, proof, vector figure `fig_idle_wall.pdf`) in `paper/sigmetrics/main.tex`; three references added to `refs.bib`.
